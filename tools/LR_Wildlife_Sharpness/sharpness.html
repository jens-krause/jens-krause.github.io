Wildlife Sharpness Analysis

Culling...
made easy.

Picks the sharpest frame out of a burst of wildlife shots automatically, so you don’t have to zoom into every one yourself. Instead of scoring the whole frame, the plug‑in weights the area the camera actually focused on, which means it gets it right more often than a plain overall sharpness score would.
It also accepts stacks as a selection: one sharpest photo gets flagged per stack, which makes it perfect for batch‑processing thousands of photos. Lightroom’s “Auto‑Stack” feature is especially handy here.

Guide

Three steps to find the sharpest shot.

Step 1

Select or stack

In the Library, select either one or more stacks, or at least two different photos. Then from the menu bar:
Library → Plug‑in Extras → Pick Sharpest Photo.

Step 2

Analysis

For each photo, the embedded preview is extracted, split into an 18×12 tile grid, and scored with a Laplacian filter. Tiles inside the camera’s autofocus area count three times as much. Depending on the burst size, this takes a few seconds.

Step 3

Result

The sharpest photo in the series automatically gets the Pick flag, all the others are left untouched.

No more manually zooming in and mashing back‑and‑forth!

Background

A plain sharpness score across the whole frame isn’t enough. In the comparison on the right, the left photo has more sharp area overall – here, because the feathers and beak provide plenty of fine edges – but the focus actually sits on the beak instead of the eye. The right photo has a shallower depth of field, yet exactly the area under the AF‑point – the eye – is sharp.

That’s why the plug‑in weights the tile covered by autofocus three times before averaging the three sharpest tiles. If the actually sharpest tiles lie outside the AF‑area – say, because the focus drifted – they can still outweigh a triple‑weighted background tile.

A small side note: the hardest part of putting this tutorial together was actually shooting a photo with the R5 II where the focus misses entirely… I built this plug‑in because I usually end up with 100+ photos of the same scene that are all sharp. Its real job is spotting the tiny differences in that sharpness ranking and flagging the single best frame out of hundreds of good ones.
